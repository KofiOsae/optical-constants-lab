Guided tutorial · From measured R/T to optical constants
Self-study course and practical laboratory guide. Follow the exercises in order; return to the Laboratory tab to run each experiment. Numerical fit quality, parameter stability, and physical validity are separate requirements.


0 · Scope: where the laboratory is useful
The Optical Constants Laboratory is designed primarily for optically uniform, laterally continuous, planar thin films and supported film stacks measured by spectral reflectance and/or transmittance. Applications include transparent coatings, absorbing semiconductor and organic films, dielectric layers, and thickness-series studies. Select the forward model and measurement geometry appropriate to the sample.
Model limitations: rough or strongly scattering films, textured substrates, anisotropic/birefringent media, graded films, patterned gratings/metasurfaces, incoherent thick layers, and strongly inhomogeneous specimens may require models beyond a coherent isotropic single-film Fresnel treatment. A good numerical fit cannot compensate for an incorrect physical model.
Before fitting, document illumination angle, polarization, spectral resolution, whether R/T are specular or total, substrate dispersion, calibration, and thickness uncertainty.


Decision guide · Is my fit good enough?
  1. Residuals: compare R/T errors to measured noise or repeatability; inspect wavelength-dependent structure, not only aggregate RMSE.
  2. Optimizer: if the evaluation budget was exhausted, increase it while keeping all else fixed. If different initial guesses give distinct solutions, investigate ambiguity.
  3. Model capacity: increase spline basis gradually. If residuals improve but n,k oscillate or change greatly, investigate overfitting, regularization and identifiability.
  4. Independent validation: fit a subset of films and predict an excluded film without using its measured R/T in training. Prediction errors should be consistent with measurement uncertainty.
  5. Measurement planning: when k and thickness trade off, measure thickness independently; when transmission saturates, include thinner films; when n,k remain correlated, consider additional angle, polarization, ellipsometry, or spectral coverage.
  6. Uncertainty: quantify parameter sensitivity and confidence/profile intervals before claiming that the optical constants are uniquely determined.
Interpretation: a converged optimizer, a stable numerical fit, predictive agreement and identified parameters are distinct achievements. Do not use one universal RMSE cutoff for all materials or instruments.


Advanced concepts · Jacobian, uncertainty and causal dispersion
The noise-weighted Jacobian contains derivatives of R/T residuals with respect to model parameters. Small singular values reveal poorly constrained parameter combinations; profile likelihoods test whether materially different parameter values can reproduce the data. These are scientific diagnostics, not a guarantee of global uniqueness.
Lorentz and Drude oscillator models describe causal dielectric dispersion; their parameters should be constrained and validated against independent measurements. A B-spline fit is flexible but does not automatically enforce Kramers–Kronig consistency. Lorentz and Drude–Lorentz joint models, Jacobian SVD summaries and excluded-film forward predictions are available in multiple-spectra mode. The Jacobian standard errors are local approximations, not global confidence intervals; profile likelihoods and validated uncertainty bands remain future work.
Hold-out protocol: train on two or more films, export n,k, then compute the forward Fresnel R/T response for a withheld film using its known thickness and substrate. Never optimize against the withheld spectrum. Compare R and T separately and repeat with different held-out films. Use the Validation: excluded film selector in multiple-spectra mode, with at least three uploaded films. The excluded film is predicted without being included in the fitting objective.


1 · Prepare and validate the optical measurement

Import a CSV, TSV, TXT, or supported JSON with wavelength and measured reflectance R and transmittance T. Confirm wavelength units (nm or µm) and whether R/T are fractions (0–1) or percentages. Use total-power R/T consistently: scattering outside the detector collection cone can invalidate a specular-film model. Record film/substrate geometry, substrate refractive index, polarization, incidence angle, and independent thickness estimates.


  1. Choose One spectrum for an initial film, or Multiple spectra to fit a shared material across different thicknesses.
  2. Upload spectra, inspect the preview and check wavelength overlap, sampling, and R/T normalization.
  3. For a passive, non-scattering stack, verify 0 ≤ R,T ≤ 1 and R+T ≤ 1 within experimental uncertainty. Absorptance is A=1−R−T when all outgoing power is collected.
  4. Enter substrate index and film thickness estimates in nm. If thickness is independently known, keep thickness fitting disabled initially.


2 · Physical theory: thin-film Fresnel inversion

The complex refractive index is N(λ)=n(λ)+ik(λ), with k≥0 for passive media under the laboratory's sign convention. A coherent-film Fresnel model combines interface reflection and propagation phase; interference fringes constrain optical thickness, while absorption damps transmission. Inverting R/T for n, k, and thickness is nonlinear and can be non-unique.


Dielectric function: ε′=n²−k² and ε″=2nk. Absorption coefficient: α=4πk/λ; intensity absorption length: 1/α. Group index: n_g=n−λ(dn/dλ). Use consistent length units. The derivative-based group index should be interpreted cautiously near strong absorption resonances.


At normal incidence, fringe spacing approximately constrains d·n_g rather than n_g or d independently. Several thicknesses, polarizations, angles, or an independent thickness measurement help break degeneracies.

4 · Understand optimizer and advanced controls

Spline basis is the number of coefficients for each of n and k: 36 basis functions imply 72 optical-constant unknowns before any fitted thicknesses. Higher basis resolves narrow bands but can introduce unstable structure. Regularization penalizes second differences of spline coefficients; 0.01 is a penalty weight, not 1% smoothing. Evaluation budget limits residual-function evaluations per least-squares solve, not necessarily the total forward-model calls in multistart or two-stage workflows.


Least squares locally refines a starting guess. Multistart repeats local fitting from alternative seeds to reduce dependence on initialization. Differential evolution explores parameters globally but can be computationally expensive in high dimensions. Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) is a stochastic global-search heuristic; HHO + least squares adds local refinement. None guarantees uniqueness or physically correct optical constants.


Fit thickness introduces additional unknowns. Joint-fit thickness tolerance restricts each thickness relative to its entered nominal value; a fitted thickness pinned at a bound is a warning, not evidence of correct thickness.


5 · Guided exercise A: a weakly absorbing film

  1. Use a simulated or measured transparent/weakly absorbing film on a known substrate; retain an independent thickness reference.
  2. Fit using Phase I → Phase II, selecting a wavelength interval with weak absorption and clear fringes.
  3. Inspect fitted R/T and residuals; export n,k and compare with the known material when available.
  4. Repeat with the spline method while keeping thickness fixed. Compare the full n,k curves, not just fit RMSE.


6 · Guided exercise B: strongly absorbing thin films — multi-sample inversion and validation

Objective: reconstruct wavelength-dependent n and k for an arbitrary strongly absorbing film using R/T spectra from two or more samples of the same material. Suitable examples include organic semiconductors, absorbing dielectric coatings, dye-doped films and semiconductors with absorption edges. The material, wavelength range, substrate, and thicknesses must be specified from the actual samples; no particular reference dataset is required.


Physical challenge: in an absorbing region, intensity transmission decreases approximately as exp(−αd), where α=4πk/λ. Once T approaches the measurement noise floor, thick samples provide little additional information about k. Thin and thick films offer complementary sensitivity, but joint fitting does not automatically guarantee uniqueness.


  1. Prepare: measure or simulate R/T for at least two thicknesses of the same material, preferably with independent thickness measurements. Check substrate, angle, polarization, wavelength units, spectral overlap, and measurement uncertainties.
  2. Establish a baseline: select multiple spectra and keep thicknesses fixed initially. Use the two-stage dispersion → spline method as a starting point; if the parametric stage fails for resonant dispersion, evaluate the spline-stage result independently.
  3. Control the fit: start with a modest spline basis, a documented regularization value, and a practical evaluation budget. Increase basis one step at a time while holding all other settings constant; record the weighted R/T RMSE, residual shape, and full n,k spectra.
  4. Check numerical stability: repeat with a larger evaluation budget or different optimizer starts. Verify termination status, thickness bounds, and whether n,k change materially between successive fits.
  5. Validate physics: check k≥0, smoothness without artificial oscillations, and plausibility of absorption features. Where available, upload independent reference n,k and compare on the overlapping wavelength grid; distinguish literature differences due to processing or sample composition.
  6. Test predictive power: fit a subset of thicknesses and predict R/T for a held-out sample. Only then enable thickness fitting with defensible tolerance bounds and assess parameter sensitivity to measurement noise.


Completion criterion: stable full-spectrum n,k across reasonable solver settings, residuals consistent with measurement uncertainty, physically plausible optical properties, and successful held-out prediction. A low R/T RMSE alone is insufficient.


7 · How to decide when a fit is stable

  1. Check solver termination: distinguish convergence from stopping at the evaluation limit. Repeat with a larger budget if needed.
  2. Check spectral residuals: inspect both R and T for structured errors, especially fringes and absorption bands.
  3. Check basis convergence: compare complete n,k curves at successive basis counts, including peak position, amplitude, RMSE and maximum deviation.
  4. Check initialization: repeat from different starts and compare solutions, not merely objective values.
  5. Check physical validity: k≥0, plausible dispersion, sensible thickness, and no unexplained oscillations. Causality/Kramers–Kronig tests require appropriate spectral coverage and extrapolation assumptions.
  6. Check prediction: hold out a film thickness or wavelength subset, fit the others, and predict the held-out R/T. Compare with an independent reference when possible.


Use application-specific tolerances justified by experimental noise; there is no universal RMSE threshold for a physically unique inversion.

9 · Reporting and reproducibility checklist

Archive original R/T files, wavelength units, geometry, substrate optical constants, nominal and fitted thicknesses, optimizer, basis, regularization, budget, fit termination status, weighted RMSE, residual plots, n,k CSV and reference source/DOI. Report numerical convergence, reference accuracy and predictive validation separately. For measured samples, document noise, calibration, scattering, surface roughness and model assumptions.


The tutorial is educational guidance; it does not certify physical uniqueness or replace independent measurements.
