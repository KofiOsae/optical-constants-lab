Guided fitting: which model should I choose?

Start with measured sample geometry and known substrate optical constants. Use Cauchy/Sellmeier for transparent films, Lorentz for isolated resonances, Voigt or KK-Gaussian for broad organic dye bands, vibronic when sidebands are resolved, and exploratory B-spline when no trustworthy line-shape model exists. Fix known thickness, set noise and mixing to zero, fit R/T, then validate recovered n,k independently. KK and SSKK are finite-band checks; they do not independently fit the dispersion. Consult the decision flow in TUTORIAL.md.

Interface mixing / roughness — where to find it?

Single: open film and substrate settings → Effective interface mixing layer; optionally enable Fit effective interface mixing. Joint: open joint specimen settings → Joint effective interface mixing. Multilayer: use each sample's Interface mixing thicknesses or its bounded fitting control. These are effective optical mixing layers, not directly measured RMS roughness or diffuse-scattering estimates.

Measurement suitability — read before fitting experimental R/T

Supported baseline: known lossless ambient and either (a) an effectively semi-infinite passive substrate (real or complex index) or (b) a finite, coherently modeled passive substrate with a lossless backing medium. The substrate must have a known complex optical index over the measured wavelength band. Transmission into a semi-infinite absorbing substrate is flux entering that substrate, not its backside detector transmission. Transparent glass or quartz may work only when their dispersion and backside effects are properly accounted for.

Not currently supported: thick-substrate incoherent backside reflections, unknown substrate dispersion, and diffuse roughness scattering. Measured R/T with any of these effects can produce biased film n,k despite optimizer convergence.

Interface mixing approximates a 50:50 dielectric mixing layer, not RMS height roughness. A lower RMSE from nonzero mixing does not establish measured roughness. For unsupported measurements, use matching numerically simulated data to validate numerical inversion, not to infer experimental material properties.

Start here · A reproducible R/T measurement
Use wavelength-dependent measured reflectance and transmittance on the same wavelength grid. Enter wavelengths in µm or nm using the declared input unit; the Laboratory displays wavelength in nm. Preview the spectra and select only a measured interval with adequate signal. Record film thickness, substrate index, incidence angle, polarization, instrument calibration and reference RI provenance.
Practical check: for passive samples, measured R and T must lie between 0 and 1. Apparent absorption is not automatically intrinsic film absorption: scattering, collection geometry and substrate losses may contribute.


Core equations · Physical quantities and units
The complex refractive index and dielectric function are related by
\[\tilde n(\lambda)=n(\lambda)+ik(\lambda),\qquad \varepsilon(\lambda)=\tilde n^2=(n^2-k^2)+i(2nk).\]
The absorption coefficient and penetration depth follow from the extinction coefficient:
\[\alpha(\lambda)=\frac{4\pi k(\lambda)}{\lambda},\qquad \delta(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\alpha(\lambda)}.\]
Use consistent length units: if wavelength is in cm, absorption coefficient is in cm⁻¹. Group index is
\[n_g(\lambda)=n(\lambda)-\lambda\frac{dn}{d\lambda}.\]
For measured and predicted reflectance and transmittance, an illustrative unweighted residual metric is
\[\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(y_j^{\mathrm{fit}}-y_j^{\mathrm{measured}})^2}.\]
The application's weighted RMSE depends on its selected channels and residual weighting; do not equate it automatically with the unweighted expression above.

How to judge whether the recovered n and k are reliable
  1. Inspect measured versus fitted R/T and the wavelength-resolved residuals, not RMSE alone.
  2. Check optimization convergence, parameter bounds, Jacobian rank and conditioning.
  3. Compare competing near-optimal branches; a low RMSE does not establish uniqueness.
  4. Where possible, predict a held-out thickness, polarization, angle or independent spectrum.
  5. Overlay literature RI only after fitting; do not use reference RI to choose a blind-inversion solution.

0 · Scope: where the laboratory is useful
The Optical Constants Laboratory is designed primarily for optically uniform, laterally continuous, planar single films on simple transparent substrates measured by spectral reflectance and/or transmittance. Applications include transparent coatings, absorbing semiconductor and organic films, dielectric layers, and thickness-series studies. Select the forward model and measurement geometry appropriate to the sample.
Model limitations: rough or strongly scattering films, textured substrates, anisotropic/birefringent media, graded films, patterned gratings/metasurfaces, incoherent thick layers, and strongly inhomogeneous specimens may require models beyond a coherent isotropic single-film Fresnel treatment. A good numerical fit cannot compensate for an incorrect physical model.
Before fitting, document illumination angle, polarization, spectral resolution, whether R/T are specular or total, substrate dispersion, calibration, and thickness uncertainty.


Decision guide · Is my fit good enough?
  1. Residuals: compare R/T errors to measured noise or repeatability; inspect wavelength-dependent structure, not only aggregate RMSE.
  2. Optimizer: if the evaluation budget was exhausted, increase it while keeping all else fixed. If different initial guesses give distinct solutions, investigate ambiguity.
  3. Model capacity: increase spline basis gradually. If residuals improve but n,k oscillate or change greatly, investigate overfitting, regularization and identifiability.
  4. Independent validation: fit a subset of films and predict an excluded film without using its measured R/T in training. Prediction errors should be consistent with measurement uncertainty.
  5. Measurement planning: when k and thickness trade off, measure thickness independently; when transmission saturates, include thinner films; when n,k remain correlated, consider additional angle, polarization, ellipsometry, or spectral coverage.
  6. Uncertainty: quantify parameter sensitivity and confidence/profile intervals before claiming that the optical constants are uniquely determined.
Interpretation: a converged optimizer, a stable numerical fit, predictive agreement and identified parameters are distinct achievements. Do not use one universal RMSE cutoff for all materials or instruments.


Organic-dye oscillator fitting · worked guidance
In the existing single-spectrum, joint-spectrum, or multilayer model dropdown select Lorentz, Modified Lorentz, KK-consistent Gaussian, Voigt or Vibronic. Oscillator resonance energies, strengths and widths are fitted within bounds. In Advanced solver settings, expand Oscillator parameters, enable customization, and supply initial, lower and upper values for each coefficient. Width parameters are energies (eV), not wavelengths (nm). With Gaussian broadening, sigma denotes the standard deviation of the resonance-energy distribution; Lorentz gamma is homogeneous damping. The model reconstructs both n and k from the complex dielectric function, not an independent Gaussian fit to k.
Worked practice: generate synthetic R/T from a 150 nm organic film on n=1.5 glass using a Voigt resonance with epsilon infinity 2.5, strength 0.8, center 2.3 eV, gamma 0.12 eV and Gaussian sigma 0.15 eV over 450–950 nm. Recover with fixed thickness, then compare one versus two oscillators and a held-out second thickness. Inspect absorption-region residuals and reference n,k agreement rather than only total RMSE. Gaussian and Voigt calculations use finite quadrature and must be checked for numerical convergence. For transparent materials Cauchy/Sellmeier presume k=0; do not use them to reproduce absorbing dye bands.
Advanced concepts · Jacobian, uncertainty and causal dispersion
The noise-weighted Jacobian contains derivatives of R/T residuals with respect to model parameters. Small singular values reveal poorly constrained parameter combinations; profile likelihoods test whether materially different parameter values can reproduce the data. These are scientific diagnostics, not a guarantee of global uniqueness.
Lorentz and Drude oscillator models describe causal dielectric dispersion; their parameters should be constrained and validated against independent measurements. A B-spline fit is flexible but does not automatically enforce Kramers–Kronig consistency. Lorentz and Drude–Lorentz joint models, Jacobian SVD summaries and excluded-film forward predictions are available in multiple-spectra mode. The Jacobian standard errors are local approximations, not global confidence intervals; profile likelihoods and validated uncertainty bands remain future work.
Hold-out protocol: train on two or more films, export n,k, then compute the forward Fresnel R/T response for a withheld film using its known thickness and substrate. Never optimize against the withheld spectrum. Compare R and T separately and repeat with different held-out films. Use the Validation: excluded film selector in multiple-spectra mode, with at least three uploaded films. The excluded film is predicted without being included in the fitting objective.


Worked exercise · Recover a single Lorentz resonance

Objective: verify the causal model against synthetic spectra with known ground-truth parameters before fitting an unknown material. This is a proposed validation protocol, not an already verified result.


For the time convention exp(−iωt), define photon energy E = 1239.841984/λ (eV, λ in nm). The one-oscillator dielectric function is ε(E) = ε∞ + f E₀²/(E₀² − E² − iΓE), with ε∞ the high-frequency background, f the dimensionless strength, E₀ the resonance energy and Γ the damping energy. Obtain N = n + ik = √ε using the passive branch k ≥ 0. The real and imaginary parts of ε are linked by the causal oscillator response; fitting n and k independently need not preserve this relationship.


  1. Construct ground truth: choose ε∞ = 2.5, f = 0.6, E₀ = 2.0 eV, Γ = 0.20 eV. These are illustrative synthetic parameters, not R6G literature values. Calculate n(λ), k(λ) from 500 to 1100 nm.
  2. Simulate R/T: use the coherent single-film Fresnel model for known 500 nm and 1000 nm films on a transparent substrate nₛ = 1.46, at normal incidence, with consistent polarization. Keep the exact forward-model conventions fixed.
  3. Fit: select Multiple spectra → Lorentz fit → 1 oscillator. Enter the known film thicknesses and disable thickness fitting. Use noise-free spectra first; then repeat with controlled independent noise and recorded random seed.
  4. Verify: compare recovered ε∞, f, E₀, Γ against the true parameters; compare reconstructed n,k and each film's R/T residuals. Inspect convergence, Jacobian sensitivity, and parameter-bound contacts.
  5. Generalization: generate an independent 2000 nm spectrum and predict it without training on its R/T. Repeat with distinct noise realizations and starting guesses.


Interpretation: small R/T errors alone do not establish parameter identifiability. Report parameter errors, wavelength-resolved residuals, and prediction errors. A second oscillator adds another (f,E₀,Γ) triplet; only introduce it if independent evidence and model comparison justify the additional degrees of freedom.


Limitations: the present Lorentz implementation uses one initial guess and a local least-squares optimizer. Its ability to recover the proposed parameters has not yet been benchmarked; multiple starts and uncertainty testing are planned. This model assumes a uniform isotropic coherent film and a real, wavelength-independent substrate index in the current interface. It is not a general multilayer or absorbing-silicon-substrate solver.


1 · Prepare and validate the optical measurement

Import a CSV, TSV, TXT, or supported JSON with wavelength and measured reflectance R and transmittance T. Confirm wavelength units (nm or µm) and whether R/T are fractions (0–1) or percentages. Use total-power R/T consistently: scattering outside the detector collection cone can invalidate a specular-film model. Record film/substrate geometry, substrate refractive index, polarization, incidence angle, and independent thickness estimates.


  1. Choose One spectrum for an initial film, or Multiple spectra to fit a shared material across different thicknesses.
  2. Upload spectra, inspect the preview and check wavelength overlap, sampling, and R/T normalization.
  3. For a passive, non-scattering stack, verify 0 ≤ R,T ≤ 1 and R+T ≤ 1 within experimental uncertainty. Absorptance is A=1−R−T when all outgoing power is collected.
  4. Enter substrate index and film thickness estimates in nm. If thickness is independently known, keep thickness fitting disabled initially.


2 · Physical theory: thin-film Fresnel inversion

The complex refractive index is N(λ)=n(λ)+ik(λ), with k≥0 for passive media under the laboratory's sign convention. A coherent-film Fresnel model combines interface reflection and propagation phase; interference fringes constrain optical thickness, while absorption damps transmission. Inverting R/T for n, k, and thickness is nonlinear and can be non-unique.


Dielectric function: ε′=n²−k² and ε″=2nk. Absorption coefficient: α=4πk/λ; intensity absorption length: 1/α. Group index: n_g=n−λ(dn/dλ). Use consistent length units. The derivative-based group index should be interpreted cautiously near strong absorption resonances.


At normal incidence, fringe spacing approximately constrains d·n_g rather than n_g or d independently. Several thicknesses, polarizations, angles, or an independent thickness measurement help break degeneracies.

4 · Understand optimizer and advanced controls

Spline basis is the number of coefficients for each of n and k: 36 basis functions imply 72 optical-constant unknowns before any fitted thicknesses. Higher basis resolves narrow bands but can introduce unstable structure. Regularization penalizes second differences of spline coefficients; 0.01 is a penalty weight, not 1% smoothing. Evaluation budget limits residual-function evaluations per least-squares solve, not necessarily the total forward-model calls in multistart or two-stage workflows.


Least squares locally refines a starting guess. Multistart repeats local fitting from alternative seeds to reduce dependence on initialization. Differential evolution explores parameters globally but can be computationally expensive in high dimensions. Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) is a stochastic global-search heuristic; HHO + least squares adds local refinement. None guarantees uniqueness or physically correct optical constants.


Fit thickness introduces additional unknowns. Joint-fit thickness tolerance restricts each thickness relative to its entered nominal value; a fitted thickness pinned at a bound is a warning, not evidence of correct thickness.


5 · Guided exercise A: a weakly absorbing film

  1. Use a simulated or measured transparent/weakly absorbing film on a known substrate; retain an independent thickness reference.
  2. Fit using Phase I → Phase II, selecting a wavelength interval with weak absorption and clear fringes.
  3. Inspect fitted R/T and residuals; export n,k and compare with the known material when available.
  4. Repeat with the spline method while keeping thickness fixed. Compare the full n,k curves, not just fit RMSE.


6 · Guided exercise B: strongly absorbing thin films — multi-sample inversion and validation

Objective: reconstruct wavelength-dependent n and k for an arbitrary strongly absorbing film using R/T spectra from two or more samples of the same material. Suitable examples include organic semiconductors, absorbing dielectric coatings, dye-doped films and semiconductors with absorption edges. The material, wavelength range, substrate, and thicknesses must be specified from the actual samples; no particular reference dataset is required.


Physical challenge: in an absorbing region, intensity transmission decreases approximately as exp(−αd), where α=4πk/λ. Once T approaches the measurement noise floor, thick samples provide little additional information about k. Thin and thick films offer complementary sensitivity, but joint fitting does not automatically guarantee uniqueness.


  1. Prepare: measure or simulate R/T for at least two thicknesses of the same material, preferably with independent thickness measurements. Check substrate, angle, polarization, wavelength units, spectral overlap, and measurement uncertainties.
  2. Establish a baseline: select multiple spectra and keep thicknesses fixed initially. Use the two-stage dispersion → spline method as a starting point; if the parametric stage fails for resonant dispersion, evaluate the spline-stage result independently.
  3. Control the fit: start with a modest spline basis, a documented regularization value, and a practical evaluation budget. Increase basis one step at a time while holding all other settings constant; record the weighted R/T RMSE, residual shape, and full n,k spectra.
  4. Check numerical stability: repeat with a larger evaluation budget or different optimizer starts. Verify termination status, thickness bounds, and whether n,k change materially between successive fits.
  5. Validate physics: check k≥0, smoothness without artificial oscillations, and plausibility of absorption features. Where available, upload independent reference n,k and compare on the overlapping wavelength grid; distinguish literature differences due to processing or sample composition.
  6. Test predictive power: fit a subset of thicknesses and predict R/T for a held-out sample. Only then enable thickness fitting with defensible tolerance bounds and assess parameter sensitivity to measurement noise.


Completion criterion: stable full-spectrum n,k across reasonable solver settings, residuals consistent with measurement uncertainty, physically plausible optical properties, and successful held-out prediction. A low R/T RMSE alone is insufficient.


7 · How to decide when a fit is stable

  1. Check solver termination: distinguish convergence from stopping at the evaluation limit. Repeat with a larger budget if needed.
  2. Check spectral residuals: inspect both R and T for structured errors, especially fringes and absorption bands.
  3. Check basis convergence: compare complete n,k curves at successive basis counts, including peak position, amplitude, RMSE and maximum deviation.
  4. Check initialization: repeat from different starts and compare solutions, not merely objective values.
  5. Check physical validity: k≥0, plausible dispersion, sensible thickness, and no unexplained oscillations. Causality/Kramers–Kronig tests require appropriate spectral coverage and extrapolation assumptions.
  6. Check prediction: hold out a film thickness or wavelength subset, fit the others, and predict the held-out R/T. Compare with an independent reference when possible.


Use application-specific tolerances justified by experimental noise; there is no universal RMSE threshold for a physically unique inversion.

9 · Reporting and reproducibility checklist

Archive original R/T files, wavelength units, geometry, substrate optical constants, nominal and fitted thicknesses, optimizer, basis, regularization, budget, fit termination status, weighted RMSE, residual plots, n,k CSV and reference source/DOI. Report numerical convergence, reference accuracy and predictive validation separately. For measured samples, document noise, calibration, scattering, surface roughness and model assumptions.


10 · Practical optical characterization without an ellipsometer

Goal: obtain defensible wavelength-dependent n and k using measured spectral reflectance R and transmittance T, rather than interpreting a small fitting error as proof of unique optical constants.


    

  1. Document the specimen. Record deposition method, independently measured thickness and its uncertainty, substrate material/thickness, roughness, homogeneity, film coverage and whether the film is anisotropic. Do not substitute a device-stack thickness for the optically measured specimen.
    2. 

  2. Document the spectrophotometer. Record whether R is specular or total hemispherical, whether an integrating sphere was used, the illumination angle, reference/baseline (air or bare substrate), polarization, spectral resolution and whether the substrate back surface contributes. These choices determine the correct forward model.
    3. 

  3. Validate the raw spectra. Use wavelength_nm,R,T or explicit wavelength_um units. Convert percentages to fractions; check wavelength order, missing data, calibration, noise and 0 ≤ R,T ≤ 1. Plot A_app=1−R−T, but call it apparent loss, not intrinsic absorption, unless all optical power channels are accounted for.
    4. 

  4. Choose the model by physics. Transparent fringe-bearing films: Swanepoel thickness/index estimates, then Cauchy or Sellmeier and Fresnel refinement. Weak absorption: use those estimates to initialize an absorbing coherent-film B-spline. Strong absorption or electronic transitions: compare constrained B-spline with Lorentz/KK-consistent dispersion. Drude terms are appropriate only with plausible free-carrier response. Cauchy and Sellmeier are not general absorbing-film models.
    5. 

  5. Constrain thickness and substrate first. Prefer independently measured film thickness and substrate n(λ). For thick transparent slides, account for incoherent backside reflections or measured baseline normalization; the semi-infinite substrate approximation is not automatically valid.
    6. 

  6. Fit and diagnose. Examine R and T separately, absolute and noise-weighted RMSE, wavelength-resolved residuals, fit termination, parameter bounds, oscillator plausibility and stability under reasonable changes in initialization, basis and regularization. A converged optimizer may reach a local minimum.
    7. 

  7. Test identifiability. Inspect Jacobian singular values and parameter correlations where available. Repeat from independent starting points. If n,k or thickness change greatly while R/T barely change, report the inversion as underconstrained rather than a unique material measurement.
    8. 

  8. Add independent information. Measure two or more film thicknesses with shared material dispersion; fit a subset and predict an excluded film. Alternative incidence angles, independent thickness measurements, and measured substrate dispersion can help. Interpolating an existing spectrum does not add independent measurements.
    9. 

  9. Assess physical consistency. Require passive-material k≥0, plausible dielectric behavior, and preferably a causal KK-consistent dispersion model. A bandgap estimate is an additional check, not a substitute for validation of n,k. Distinguish absorption from scattering and collection losses.
    10. 

  10. Report the evidence level. (A) Numerical convergence; (B) measured-spectrum reconstruction; (C) stability and local identifiability; (D) independent prediction or reference agreement. Only claim the levels actually demonstrated. Include uncertainty and wavelength ranges where the result is reliable.
    11. 



Worked decision example — absorbing BiSI on glass: with 150 nm nominal thickness, measure R/T from 300–1400 nm, first inspect residuals for both channels. A broad reflectance mismatch near 780 nm is not removed merely by declaring optimizer convergence. Check substrate reference/backside, roughness/scattering and absorption-edge dispersion before claiming intrinsic n,k. This illustrates model adequacy, not a universal BiSI prescription.


Method comparison: use identical measured data and geometry when comparing Swanepoel-initialized B-spline, direct B-spline and classical dispersion fits. Compare predictive spectra and stability, not only training RMSE.
