Laboratory guide · Theory, methods and examples
Guided spectral analysis: from R/T to optical constants
Use this guide alongside the Reconstruction Lab. Start with the measured spectrum, check its physical consistency, then choose an inverse model. These diagnostics do not by themselves establish a unique n,k solution.
Step 1 · Read the measured spectrum
Reflectance R is the reflected fraction of incident power; transmittance T is the transmitted fraction. For a passive sample with properly calibrated total-power measurements, absorptance is A=1−R−T. A negative value beyond measurement uncertainty indicates a calibration, normalization, or model problem. Absorbance is defined as −log10(T), which is not the same as absorptance.
Look for: oscillating interference fringes (optical thickness), a falling transmission envelope (possible absorption), a rapid absorption edge, and discontinuities or spikes (potential instrument artifacts). Fringes alone cannot uniquely determine both thickness and refractive index.
Step 2 · Prepare the data and identify the structure
Confirm wavelength units (nm or µm), R/T units (fraction or percent), monotonically increasing wavelength, and whether R and T were measured at the same angles and polarization. Record the sample's layer order, independently measured film thickness, substrate material, and whether the substrate backside contributes to the measurement. Do not use a semi-infinite-substrate model for data with significant backside interference without accounting for that effect.
For FMM/TMM simulations, export wavelength, R, T, known film n and k (kept separate from inversion), layer thicknesses, substrate dispersion, angle, polarization, and the definition of transmitted power. For periodic gratings, diffraction-order power must be treated separately from the uniform-film model.
Step 3 · Understand the equations before fitting
The complex refractive index is N(λ)=n(λ)+i k(λ), using exp(−iωt). The absorption coefficient is α(λ)=4πk(λ)/λ. The optical phase accumulated through a film at normal incidence is δ(λ)=2πn(λ)d/λ. In a simple lossless normal-incidence interface, R=[(n₁−n₂)/(n₁+n₂)]². For a coherent film, multiple reflected waves interfere, so R and T require the Fresnel/transfer-matrix forward model rather than a direct Beer–Lambert inversion.
When a single R/T spectrum fits well, that establishes spectral agreement—not necessarily the uniqueness of n, k, or thickness. A physically admissible solution also requires k≥0, appropriate dispersion behavior, and acceptable uncertainty.
Step 4 · Choose the extraction method
Swanepoel: useful when well-resolved transmission extrema exist and its transparent-film assumptions are approximately satisfied. Blind absorption-aware initialization: estimates starting n and k from R/T, then refines them with a coherent-film forward model; the Beer–Lambert step is an initializer only. Spline inversion: fits wavelength-dependent n and k, with regularization to discourage unstable oscillations. Joint inversion: fits one shared n,k to several spectra of the same material with separate thicknesses or measurement geometries.
Step 5 · Diagnose the reconstructed result
Compare measured and fitted R/T separately. Plot residuals Rfit−Rmeasured and Tfit−Tmeasured versus wavelength, rather than relying only on a single RMSE. Systematic fringe shifts suggest incorrect optical thickness; structured residuals near an absorption edge may indicate inadequate dispersion or absorption modeling; large residuals near the substrate response may indicate a wrong substrate model. Repeat the fit from independent starting points and plausible thicknesses, and compare recovered n,k against independent references where available.
Step 6 · Worked FMM experiment: three thicknesses, one material
Simulate one uniform dispersive film on the same substrate at d=500, 1000, and 2000 nm over a shared wavelength interval. Export three R/T files and a separate known-n,k reference. First fit each file individually; then upload all three in the Multiple spectra workflow with their correct thicknesses. Compare the shared recovered n,k to the withheld reference using MAE(n), MAE(k), and per-spectrum R/T RMSE. Repeat after adding controlled measurement noise. A better spectral RMSE without improved n,k accuracy does not count as successful material recovery.

2 · Physical structure and Fresnel forward model
For a single coherent film of complex index N=n+ik and thickness d between ambient n₀ and semi-infinite substrate nₛ, define δ=2πNd/λ, r₀₁=(n₀−N)/(n₀+N), r₁₂=(N−nₛ)/(N+nₛ). At normal incidence:
r = (r₀₁+r₁₂ exp(2iδ))/(1+r₀₁r₁₂ exp(2iδ))
t = t₀₁t₁₂ exp(iδ)/(1+r₀₁r₁₂ exp(2iδ))
R=|r|²; T=Re(nₛ/n₀)|t|² for lossless ambient and substrate. The fitted film must match the sample geometry. The current inverse model does not include backside reflections from a finite glass slab.


3 · B-spline inversion: what the optimizer actually fits
Represent n(λ)=Σ aⱼBⱼ(λ) and k(λ)=Σ bⱼBⱼ(λ). At each trial set of coefficients, the Fresnel model predicts R and T. The solver minimizes weighted R/T differences plus a second-difference smoothness penalty on spline coefficients. The fitted thickness may also vary within specified bounds.
Least squares is local. Multistart, differential evolution and HHO explore alternative initial solutions. More basis functions can fit noise and increase nonuniqueness. Smooth B-splines do not automatically satisfy Kramers–Kronig relations.


4 · Swanepoel: transmission-envelope extraction
For a sufficiently transparent film on a known transparent substrate with resolved interference fringes, construct upper and lower transmission envelopes Tₘₐₓ and Tₘᵢₙ. Let Q=2s(Tmax−Tmin)/(Tmax Tmin)+(s²+1)/2. Then n=[Q+√(Q²−s²)]¹ᐟ². Fringe spacings can also constrain thickness. Use the Classical Methods tab with an uploaded transmission spectrum and substrate index s>1. If fringes are absent, the method is not applicable; do not force a numerical answer.


5 · Absorption, Tauc and optical gap
For passive N=n+ik, the absorption coefficient is α=4πk/λ. If decadic absorbance A₁₀ is known and interface/scattering effects are negligible, α≈ln(10) A₁₀/d. For a Tauc exponent p, plot (αhν)^p versus photon energy hν and fit a physically justified linear absorption-edge interval. Extrapolate its fitted line to zero for an apparent optical gap. Choose p from the assumed transition model; fitting any arbitrary interval is not a defensible bandgap measurement.


6 · Lorentz, Drude and Kramers–Kronig: scope
A causal Lorentz oscillator uses ε(ω)=ε∞+Σ fⱼ/(ωⱼ²−ω²−iγⱼω); Drude adds a free-carrier term. With ε=(n+ik)², real and imaginary dielectric responses are linked by causality through Kramers–Kronig relations. The present B-spline fit is not KK-constrained. Dedicated causal-model inverse fitting remains to be validated and should not be confused with a smooth spline reconstruction.


7 · Ellipsometry and multilayer limitations
Ellipsometry measures the complex reflection ratio ρ=rₚ/rₛ=tan(Ψ)exp(iΔ). The repository includes a coherent multilayer forward model, but a validated ellipsometric inverse workflow is not yet exposed here. A finite-thickness substrate may require incoherent averaging or mixed coherent–incoherent treatment, which is distinct from a semi-infinite substrate.


8 · Validation and common failure diagnoses
  • Upload error: confirm CSV headers, wavelength units, R/T fractions versus percent, and the selected file type.
  • Flat or implausible n,k: check sample structure, thickness estimate, signal normalization, spectral range and whether R and T constrain the problem.
  • Swanepoel fails: inspect whether at least two resolved maxima and minima are present.
  • Low RMSE but wrong n,k: test alternative thickness starts, fewer spline coefficients, independent reference data or multiple sample thicknesses.
  • Reference R6G fails: it is optional and must not block custom experimental data.
